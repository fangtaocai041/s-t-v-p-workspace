Background Species-specific or health status specific microbiome composition of cetaceans is still poorly classified due to the limited samples. Despite a partial identification of the gut microbiota of melon-headed whales ( Peponocephala electra ), comparative analyses across anatomical systems are lacking. This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of the microbial communities habiting nine body sites - oral cavity, esophagus, foregut, midgut, hindgut, blowhole, and skin wounds (left anterior, dorsal fin, tail) - in a stranded melon-headed whale. Results By 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, a decrease in microbial richness was observed from the oral cavity to the hindgut, accompanied by compositional shifts from Fusobacterium-dominated oral/esophageal niches to Vibrio -enriched gastrointestinal regions (21.81% Vibrio , 21.19% Fusobacterium , 12.50% Actinobacillus ). The respiratory tract microbiota underwent a significant shift and was dominated by Ostreibacterium (57.5%), Helcococcus (6.59%), and Tenacibaculum (4.12%). Skin wounds showed environmental similarities, with Vibrio (47.84%), Pseudoalteromonas (17.84%), and Psychrobacter (12.36%). Pan-microbiome screening identified seven Vibrio species ( V. alginolyticus , V. cidicii , V. cyclitrophicus , V. navarrensis , V. parahaemolyticus , V. salilacus , and V. splendidus ) across all niches, along with V. cholerae in non-respiratory samples. Notably, V. profundi was exclusively localized to anterior and dorsal fin wounds. Functional profiling revealed enrichment of Vibrio -linked pathogenesis pathways (infection, pathogenic cycle) and metabolic modules that were correlated with immunocompromised states. Conclusions This study revealed significant bidirectional environment-host microbiome exchange dynamics across cetacean mucosal surfaces. Notably, Vibrio spp. emerged as the dominant genus in both gastrointestinal and cutaneous wound microbiomes, highlighting: (1) potential zoonotic transmission risks from pathogenic Vibrio strains, and (2) the critical need for habitat-specific microbial surveillance to inform marine mammal conservation strategies. These microorganisms reach densities of 10 6 −10 7 cells/mL in the 1.3 billion km³ of seawater on Earth, where they drive critical biogeochemical processes. Microorganisms associated with marine animals are part of the animal’s microbiomes, which are collections of microorganisms residing on or within the hosts. Microbiomes can impact the health, physiology, behavior, and ecology of marine animals. This has the potential to change our understanding of how animals adapt to environmental changes, especially with the growing impact of climate change and human activities on marine ecosystems [ 1 ]. The melon-headed whale ( Peponocephala electra ), a member of the Delphinidae family, is widely distributed in the tropical and subtropical ocean [ 2 ]. The ecology of melon-headed whales has been a significant area of biological research for a long time. As top predators, melon-headed whales indirectly influence the distribution of marine microorganisms by controlling the abundance of prey. Their feeding habits and subsequent defecation contribute to the vertical flux of nutrients such as urea, ammonia, and dissolved iron into the photic zone [ 3 ]. These metabolites act as bioavailable nitrogen, phosphorus, and trace metal sources, stimulating primary productivity in phytoplankton communities and shaping the composition of heterotrophic bacterioplankton dependent on organic matter remineralization [ 4 ]. Despite their ecological significant and widespread presence, the microbiota associated with melon-headed whales remains poorly understood. Microbial communities play a crucial role in host health by affecting key life processes of the body such as digestion, immunity, and disease resistance [ 5 ]. Understanding the microbiome of melon-headed whales is crucial in gaining insight into their health status, ecological interactions, and conservation needs. Current research on cetacean microbiomes remains taxonomically constrained, with most studies concentrating on just a handful of species. While over 90 cetacean species have been documented, comprehensive microbiome analyses across different anatomical sites-including skin, gastrointestinal tract, and respiratory system-have only been conducted for a limited number [ 6 – 20 ]. Notably, among the few species with multi-niche microbiome characterization, the melon-headed whale stands out since only gut microbiota has been examined. This narrow focus significantly restricts our understanding of whole-body microbial composition and health assessment in this species. Distinct biogeographic patterns emerge across marine habitats, with coastal zones, pelagic waters, and abyssal regions each harboring unique microbial communities that have adapted to their respective environments [ 21 ]. Previous research indicates that all those environments were also abundant in pathogenic bacteria, adversely harming marine species across several taxa [ 22 ]. Marine vertebrates, particularly cetaceans and teleosts, face unique challenges in wound healing due to their constant exposure to pathogens in the water. Skin lesions in cetaceans arise from biotic interactions (e.g., predator attacks), anthropogenic impacts (fishing gear abrasions, vessel collisions), and abiotic stressors. The constant exposure to seawater containing elevated bacterial loads creates persistent infection risks, potentially disrupting healing processes and leading to secondary systemic diseases [ 23 , 24 ]. The role of microbes in wound healing in marine mammals is multi-dimensional. While opportunistic pathogens, such as Vibrio vulnificus , invade wounds to establish localized infections that delay tissue regeneration, the commensal microbiota may competitively exclude invaders through niche occupation and antimicrobial metabolite production [ 25 ]. This delicate ecological balance between pathogenic and beneficial microbial interactions plays a critical role in determining the healing outcomes in aquatic organisms [ 26 ]. The mutual impact of the host and the environment on the microbiome is also crucial in shaping microbial communities in marine ecosystems. While direct contact and physiological activities such as defecation and skin shedding are important pathways for microbial dispersal, environmental reservoirs (e.g., seawater microbiota) and host immune filtering mechanisms may reciprocally modulate microbial exchange dynamics [ 1 ]. Host-associated microbes released into the environment can influence local microbial communities and biogeochemical cycling [ 3 ]. Conversely, the ocean environment exerts selective pressure on cetacean-associated microbiomes as seawater harbors a dynamic and diverse microbial pool that is influenced by salinity, temperature, pollutants, and other abiotic factors [ 27 ]. Environmental microbes may colonize cetaceans’ open systems, such as the skin or respiratory tract, that are directly exposed to seawater [ 26 ]. Consequently, elucidating the pathogenic microbiota associated with cetaceans holds significance not only for host species conservation but also provides critical insights into marine pathogen biogeography and epidemiological dynamics. This is particularly evident in highly mobile cetacean species such as the melon-headed whale ( Peponocephala electra ), whose extensive migratory ranges facilitate cross-ecosystem microbial transmission. In this study, we characterize the specific microbial communities found in different anatomical niches of a melon-headed whale ( Peponocephala electra ). Samples were taken from the gastrointestinal tract, including the skin wounds, oral cavity, esophagus, blowhole and the luminal contents of the foregut, midgut, and hindgut. With data from different anatomical niches, we tried to address two fundamental questions: (1) What is the composition of the melon-headed whale’s microbiota? (2) What are the primary pathogenic bacteria found in melon-headed whales? This work provides novel insights into the cetacean microbiome community structure while advancing the emerging field of marine microbiomes. Meanwhile, A specialized focus on wound-associated microbiota further explains the microbial community succession patterns in marine integumentary injury repair. Methods Sample collection Microbiome samples were collected from a stranded melon-headed whale ( Peponocephala electra ) near Longtian, Shantou, Guangdong, China (23.3 °N, 116.7 °E) in January 2023. Its appearance is described in Fig. 1 . The melon-headed whale was in a weakened state when transferred to the aquarium for rescue but ultimately did not survive. Microbiota sampling was conducted immediately after death confirmation. To minimize postmortem microbial changes, all samples - including those from the gastrointestinal tract (oral cavity, esophagus, foregut, midgut, hindgut), respiratory tract (blowhole), skin wounds (left anterior, dorsal fin, tail), and surrounding seawater - were collected within 90 min. The carcass met the Code 1 necropsy criteria, which include a postmortem interval of 24 h or less, absence of bloating or putrefactive odor, limited skin sloughing, and preserved ocular/mucosal integrity. The morphometric analysis involved total body length measurement for age estimation [ 28 ], with nutritional status assessed via blubber thickness at sternal and caudodorsal anatomical landmarks [ 29 ]. Fig. 1 Location and image of the stranded melon-headed whale Microbiota samples were collected according to established protocols for cetacean microbiome studies. Digestive tract specimens included mucosal swabs from the oral cavity, esophagus, and lumen of the foregut, midgut, and hindgut. The respiratory system sampling targeted the blowhole epithelium, while the integumentary samples consisted of wound exudates from the left anterior, dorsal fin, and tail regions. The seawater from the stranding site was used as environmental control [ 17 ]. Biological triplicates ( n ≥ 3 per site) were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. The sample collection was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee in Shantou University by project “Possible causes of death of marine mammals, from 2022.01-2077.12”. Genomic DNA extraction Genomic DNA was isolated from collected samples using the MagPure Stool DNA KF Kit B (MAGEN, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, swab tips containing biological samples were transferred into bead-containing centrifuge tubes, where they were homogenized with 1 mL of Buffer ATL/PVP-10 using a commercial grinding apparatus (Shanghai Jingxin Tech., China). Following a 20-minute incubation at 65 °C, the lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 min (Eppendorf, Germany). The resulting supernatant was subjected to PCI buffer extraction (0.6 mL) with 15-second vortex mixing, followed by centrifugation at 18,213 × g for 10 min. Transfer the sample to the corresponding location in the deep-well plate of the Kingfisher machine (Thermo Fisher, USA). Start the MagPure_Stool_DNA_KF program on the Kingfisher machine. Transfer the DNA to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube for storage once the program has finished running. Genomic DNA quality was checked by using Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit with Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Library construction and sequencing The Library was prepared using 2 × Phanta Max Master Mix (VAZYME, China) polymerase, and the V3V4 variable region of 16 S rDNA of bacteria was amplified by forward and reverse PCR degenerate primers F and R (338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’)). The process of PCR enrichment was executed within a 50-microliter reaction, comprising 30 nanograms of template DNA and fusion PCR primers. The PCR cycling conditions that were employed are outlined below: The initial step in the protocol entailed an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min. After this, 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 56 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s were performed. The final extension step was carried out at a temperature 72 °C for 5 min. The purification of PCR products was achieved using DNA magnetic beads (BGI, LB00V60). Subsequently, the final double-strand library products undergo a denaturation process, resulting in the generation of single-strand library products. The circularization reaction is then initiated to produce single-strand circularized DNA products. The goal of this study is to determine if a single strand of linear DNA can be digested to remove it. The final circularized library is amplified using the phi29 and rolling circle amplification (RCA) methods to generate the DNA nano ball (DNB), which carries multiple copies of the initial single-stranded library molecule. The DNBs are then loaded into a patterned nanoarray, and sequencing reads of PE300/250 bases are generated using the DNBSEQ-100 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). Sequencing data processing After collecting raw sequencing data, a rigorous quality control (QC) procedure was implemented. Cutadapt (v2.6) was used to computationally trim adapter sequences. Reads with an average Phred quality value lower than 20 over a 30 bases pair sliding window will be truncated. The removal of reads whose lengths are 75% of their original lengths is necessary after truncation via Readfq (v1.0). Paired-end reads were merged into contigs using FLASH (v1.2.11), with the following parameters: a minimum overlap of 15 base pairs (bp) and a maximum mismatch tolerance of 10% [ 30 ]. Chimeric sequences were identified through a reference-based alignment process against the SILVA 138 SSU Ref NR99 database, using the UCHIME (v4.2.40) algorithm in de novo mode [ 31 ]. High-quality sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% nucleotide similarity using USEARCH (v7.0.1090) with default parameters [ 32 ]. Representative sequences from each OTU were then taxonomically classified using the RDP classifier, comparing sequences against the Ribosomal Database Project reference database (RDP) with a confidence cutoff of 0.6 [ 33 ]. Microbiome diversity and structure comparison The analysis of microbial diversity and community structure was conducted using a combination of α- and β-diversity metrics. To mitigate potential biases arising from differential sequencing depths, raw counts were normalized to relative abundances using total sum scaling prior to diversity metric calculations. The α-diversity was quantified by two methods: the observed species richness (Sobs) and the Chao1 estimator for asymptotic richness. The assessment of β-diversity was conducted through the utilization of weighted UniFrac distances, a method that quantifies phylogenetic dissimilarity among microbial communities. Computational workflows were implemented in MOTHUR (v.1.32.2) and QIIME (v1.80). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Weight UniFrac distances was performed to assess the bacterial taxonomic composition of the samples. A permutational analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) was conducted to ascertain whether there were significant differences ( p &lt; 0.05) in community structure across anatomical sampling sites. Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt2) is a computational method that predicts the function abundance of microbial communities based on marker gene sequencing profiles. A comparison of PICRUSt1 and PICRUSt2 shows that the latter is more accurate and has an expanded database of gene families and reference genomes. Heatmap of pathogens identified by the organism The taxonomic composition was profiled through a clustered heatmap (ggplot2 v4.1.3) displaying log10(x*10^2)-transformed relative abundances, where x represents the proportion of each operational taxonomic unit (OTU) [ 34 ]. Zero-abundance values were imputed using log10(x min *10^2) defined as the minimum non-zero abundance across all samples. Statistical analyses Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.2; Vienna, Austria). Pairwise comparisons of α-diversity metrics between anatomical regions employed the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U implementation in the stats package), with significance defined at p &lt; 0.05. To address the issue of multiplicity inflation in family-wise error rates, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (α = 0.05) via the p.adjust function. Results Subsection A total of 5,599,740 high-quality paired end reads were generated across 60 samples from the digestive tract (oral, esophagus, foregut, midgut, hindgut), respiratory system (blowhole), integumentary (left anterior, dorsal fin, tail wounds), and environmental (water) matrices, yielding a mean of 77,723 reads per sample (Supplementary Table S1 ). Following the implementation of quality filtering and chimera removal, in total 2,976,827 sequences were classified into 1437 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% nucleotide similarity using USEARCH (v10.0.240), using taxonomic classification against the Greengenes 13_8 reference database (16 S rRNA V3-V4 region) (Supplementary Table S2 ). The species accumulation curves suggest that the current sequencing depth is sufficient for identifying taxa (Supplementary Figure s1 ). Microbial richness analysis revealed significantly higher SOBS values in the oral cavity, blowhole, dorsal fin wound, tail wound, and surrounding water compared to esophageal and gut samples ( p &lt; 0.05), a pattern confirmed by Chao1 index measurements (Fig. 2 A, B). Along the gastrointestinal tract, we observed a progressive richness decline from oral cavity (Sobs = 364 ± 16; Chao1 = 531 ± 53) to esophagus (Sobs = 238 ± 19; Chao1 = 362 ± 63), followed by foregut (Sobs = 210 ± 14; Chao1 = 349 ± 23), midgut (Sobs = 209 ± 13; Chao1 = 320 ± 28), and hindgut (Sobs = 186 ± 12; Chao1 = 286 ± 30). Notably, skin wounds exhibited substantial variation, with tail lesions showing the highest richness (Sobs = 566 ± 71; Chao1 = 764 ± 70) and left anterior wounds the lowest (Sobs = 195 ± 30; Chao1 = 300 ± 55). Environmentally exposed niches (oral, blowhole, dorsal fin wounds) displayed richness levels comparable to water samples, while tail wounds emerged as the richest anatomical site overall (Fig. 7 A, B). As for Shannon and Simpson diversity indices supported these observations (Fig. 2 C, D), the oral cavity (2.94 ± 0.23), blowhole (3.31 ± 0.17), and tail wound (3.29 ± 0.30) showed peak Shannon diversity, contrasting with lower values in gut regions (foregut: 1.77 ± 0.02; midgut: 2.35 ± 0.06; hindgut: 2.26 ± 0.03) and left anterior wounds (2.13 ± 0.10). Simpson analysis indicated strongest dominance in the blowhole (0.08 ± 0.01), potentially reflecting environmental influences, while the foregut showed maximal evenness (0.24 ± 0.01). PCoA ( A ) and NMDS ( B ) revealed that microbiota varied with body sites and clustered by major habitats ANOSIM tests revealed significant overall differences in microbial composition between sample types ( R = 0.698, p = 0.001). Interestingly, even within the gastrointestinal tract, we observed strong compartmentalization - foregut, midgut, and hindgut communities were all significantly distinct from each other ( R = 1, p = 0.001). Wound and water samples showed moderate clustering overlap ( R = 0.014, p = 0.408), suggesting phylogenetic similarity. In contrast, respiratory tract microbiomes formed a distinct cluster ( R = 0.781 vs. gastrointestinal, p = 0.001; R = 1 vs. wound, p = 0.001; R = 1 vs. water, p = 0.003), highlighting a unique microbial composition when compared to other anatomical sites (Figure s2 ). Microbial taxonomic profiles To explore the composition of microbiome across different anatomical and environmental niches, taxonomic profiling was performed. Eight bacterial phyla, including Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, Cyanobacteriota, Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, Fusobacteriota, Campylobacterota, and the candidate phylum SR1, were detected. Fusobacteriota and Pseudomonadota were the top 2 dominant phyla in all anatomical niches (Fig. 4 A). Meanwhile, high complexity of microbiome composition was found in Order level (Fig. 4 B). Fig. 4 Relative abundance of the microbiome from the gastrointestinal tract, respiratory tract, wound, and water of a melon-headed whale at the phylum level ( A ), order level ( B ), and genus level ( C ) The gastrointestinal tract microbiomes showed gradual alterations in composition, with the foregut dominated by Vibrionales (42.56 ± 8.54%) and Pasteurellales (34.34 ± 5.28%), transitioning to midgut dominance of Fusobacteriales (39.21 ± 4.35%) and Eubacteriales (26.34 ± 2.95%), before stabilizing in the hindgut with Vibrionales resurgence (50.66 ± 1.23%) and persistent Fusobacteriales (29.08 ± 1.41%). The upper digestive niches diverged significantly, with oral and esophageal microbiomes having high Fusobacteriales abundance (28.41 ± 6.91% and 30.61 ± 1.84%, respectively) but with differences in secondary taxa: Bacteroidales dominated oral communities (23.06 ± 6.50%), while the esophagus showed elevated Pasteurellales (20.14 ± 1.24%). The respiratory (blowhole) and wound samples showed distinct adaptations. The blowhole microbiome showed a specialization of Cardiobacteriales (57.46 ± 0.96%), whereas wound sites showed a predominance of Vibrionales and Alteromonadales (left anterior: 75.94 ± 9.70% and 21.74 ± 10.24%, respectively; tail: 51.34 ± 6.95% and 22.76 ± 5.89%, respectively). The dorsal fin wounds showed transitional communities with Pseudomonadales dominance (37.71 ± 13.46%). The environmental seawater microbiomes reflected the wound-associated patterns (Vibrionales: 63.10 ± 5.65%; Alteromonadales: 17.55 ± 1.70%), suggesting aqueous microbial seeding of superficial tissues. Interestingly, Oceanospirillales, which are rarely found in host-associated niches, made up 11.68 ± 1.47% of the aquatic microbiota, highlighting marine-specific microbial recruitment. Distinct genus-level microbial characteristics were observed across different anatomical and environmental niches, reflecting both host adaptation and environmental exposure (Fig. 4 C). The gastrointestinal tract microbiomes showed a longitudinal pattern with Vibrio dominating the foregut and hindgut communities (40.78 ± 8.68% and 49.46 ± 1.23%, respectively) but declining progressively to 3.79 ± 0.33% in the midgut, inversely correlating with Fusobacterium , which increased from undetectable levels in the foregut to 39.02 ± 4.33% and 27.81 ± 1.63% in the midgut and hindgut, respectively. The upper digestive niches diverged significantly, with oral microbiomes characterized by oral biofilm formers Porphyromonas (20.26 ± 6.97%) and Fusobacterium (19.98 ± 6.74%), while esophageal communities shifted toward Fusobacterium (20.08% ± 1.48%) and Actinobacillus (19.03 ± 1.34%). The wound microbiomes showed environmental convergence, with a dominant presence of aquatic genera Vibrio (left anterior: 73.91 ± 9.28%; tail: 49.94 ± 6.84%; dorsal fin: 21.02 ± 12.53%) and Pseudoalteromonas (left anterior: 20.74 ± 10.53%; tail: 19.66 ± 5.28%; dorsal fin: 13.36 ± 4.38%). Interestingly, Marinomonas was higher in seawater samples (8.65 ± 1.69%) than host niches (&lt; 0.67% in wounds), whereas Psychrobacter were predominant only in dorsal fin wounds (36.51 ± 14.27%). The respiratory (blowhole) microbiomes showed distinct specialization, with Ostreibacterium (57.43 ± 2.79%) and Helcococcus (6.58 ± 1.19%) absent from other sites. This niche specificity is sharply contrasted with the environmental coherence observed in integumentary systems. Nevertheless, our findings provide preliminary insights into potential pathogen composition in marine mammals, which may inform future investigations. To characterize the community composition and spatial distribution of pathogenic microorganisms across sampling sites, we analyzed OTU results to identify potential pathogens and compare their relative abundances. The results of this study are depicted in Fig. 5 . Higher abundance of Vibrio alginolyticus , Vibrio cholerae and Vibrio parahaemolyticus was found in the foregut, hindgut, left anterior wound, tail wound, and water samples. Wound sites (left anterior, dorsal fin, and tail) and water samples were particularly enriched with Vibrio salilacus , Vibrio splendidus , and Vibrio cyclitrophicus . Vibrio cidicii was enriched in the foregut, hindgut, left anterior wound, and water samples while Vibrio navarrensis was predominantly enriched in the foregut and hindgut. And Vibrio profundi was detected at higher levels in the left anterior and dorsal fin wounds. The analysis revealed clear niche preferences among bacterial taxa ( p &lt; 0.05; Supplementary Table S3 ). Pseudoalteromonas tetraodonis was predominated in the wound and water samples while Actinobacillus scotiae and Porphyromonas pasteri were concentrated in the esophagus and foregut. Oral samples showed specific enrichment of Oceanivirga salmonicida . Photobacterium damselae displayed broad distribution across gut regions and tail wounds. Gut-associated pathogens including Fusobacterium mortiferum , Bacteroides fragilis , Morganella morganii , Enterococcus faecalis , and Aeromonas hydrophila were particularly abundant in midgut/hindgut samples, with E. faecalis also present in tail wounds. Two exceptions to these patterns were observed: Acinetobacter lwoffii showed no site-specific variation, while Butyricimonas faecalis maintained consistently low abundance across all samples. Pathogenic members dominated across sample types, with V. alginolyticus and V. parahaemolyticus , known agents of vibriosis, collectively accounting for 10.50 ± 10.56% and 5.40 ± 5.01% mean relative abundances, respectively. The environmental and wound-associated niches showed elevated abundances of V. salilacus (1.84 ± 1.02% in wounds; 2.31% in seawater) and V. splendidus (7.97 ± 2.10% in wounds; 16.60% in seawater), the latter being a recognized opportunistic pathogen in aquaculture systems [ 35 ]. Notably, V. cyclitrophicus , linked to temperature-dependent mussel mortality [ 36 ], and V. navarrensis , a potential human pathogen [ 37 ], were predominantly found in aquatic environments. Conversely, the gastrointestinal and respiratory microbiomes had low Vibrio diversity, while wounds and seawater had a high Vibrio predominance (Fig. 5 ). This niche-specific distribution pattern suggests that aquatic transmission routes drive integumentary colonization, emphasizing the critical relationship between environmental reservoirs and host-pathogen dynamics. The cholera-causing V. cholerae , although detected, showed limited prevalence across niches, possibly due to host-specific adaptation barriers. Our study revealed distinct patterns of potential pathogen colonization in different niches, with important ecological and clinical implications. Pseudoalteromonas tetraodonis , a pathogen implicated in echinoderm skin ulcers and epizootic outbreaks [ 38 ], was significantly enriched in wound (17.53 ± 3.12%), and surrounding seawater samples (11.24%) compared to gastrointestinal (0.06 ± 0.01%) and respiratory tract niches (0.18%) samples. In contrast, Actinobacillus scotiae , a septicemia-associated pathogen [ 39 ], preferentially colonized the gastrointestinal tract (11.92 ± 13.04%), with minimal abundance in the respiratory tracts (0.20%), wounds (0.10 ± 0.04%) and seawater (0.21%). The digestive tract had specialized microbial communities, with Photobacterium damselae , the causative agent of fulminant wound-associated septicemia [ 40 ], showing progressive enrichment from foregut (1.77%) to midgut (12.75%) and hindgut (1.20%). Two intestinal pathogens demonstrated posterior gut specialization: Fusobacterium mortiferum , associated with hemorrhagic ulcers and systemic inflammation [ 41 , 42 ], peaked in the midgut (3.34%) and hindgut (2.38%). Bacteroides fragilis , linked to inflammatory bowel pathologies [ 20 ], showed similar distribution patterns (midgut: 3.89%; hindgut: 1.86%). Upper digestive tract specialists included Porphyromonas pasteri , a potential indicator of microbial imbalance [ 43 ], which was mainly detected in blowhole (0.24%), oral (0.34%), and esophageal (7.83%) niches. The percentage of the pathogen was lower in other samples (less than 0.06%). Similarly, Oceanivirga salmonicida , a marine pathogen responsible for salmonid epidemics [ 19 ], was found to concentrate in oral (5.56%) and esophageal (0.47%) samples. The pathogen percentage was less than 0.02% in other samples. Low-abundance taxa that were widespread across all sites (&lt; 0.05% relative abundance across all sites) included Acinetobacter lwoffii , Morganella morganii , Enterococcus faecalis , Aeromonas hydrophila , and Butyricimonas faecalis . Predicted functions of microbiota across different body habitats To elucidate the functional potential of the melon-headed whale microbiota across different anatomical niches, we performed metagenomic functional prediction using the updated PICRUSt2 pipeline. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Level 1 pathway analysis revealed significant conserved enrichment of core metabolic processes - Metabolism (77.87 ± 0.9%), Genetic Information Processing (11.98 ± 1.48%), and Cellular Processes (5.35 ± 1.03%) - across all body habitats and environmental seawater. The Human Diseases pathway showed notable variation, with the highest relative abundance in wounds (1.4 ± 0.29%) and water (1.67%), followed by gastrointestinal tracts (1.29 ± 0.25%), which is associated with elevated pathogen prevalence in these niches (Fig. 6 ). The results indicate that the microbiota of wound and gut is composed of more potential pathogens. KEGG Level 2 pathway analysis revealed specific functional signatures across anatomical and environmental microbiomes. Gastrointestinal tract microbiota was enriched for metabolic processes including ansamycin biosynthesis, D-glutamine/D-glutamate metabolism, vancomycin-group antibiotic biosynthesis, biotin metabolism, and peptidoglycan biosynthesis. Respiratory tract communities shared gastrointestinal features in ansamycin and vancomycin-related biosynthesis but diverged through the dominance of bacterial chemotaxis, branched-chain amino acid (valine/leucine/isoleucine) biosynthesis, and C5-branched dibasic acid metabolism. The skin wound microbiomes exhibited hybrid functionalities, combining ansamycin biosynthesis and branched-chain amino acid metabolism with fatty acid biosynthesis, flagellar assembly, and pentose phosphate pathway activation. In the microbiome derived from the surrounding seawater environment, functional potential analysis revealed elevated carbohydrate metabolism. This observation may be attributed to the higher proportion of heterotrophic bacteria, particularly Alteromonadales, present in the samples. Fig. 6 Functional profile of the microbial communities from the gastrointestinal tract, respiratory tract, wound, and water of a melon-headed whale predicted by PICRUSt2 Discussion The microbiota plays a dual role as both an influence on host health-disease dynamics and a biomarker for physiological assessment. Research on cetacean microbial communities, particularly those inhabiting the gastrointestinal system, respiratory tract, integumentary lesions, and aquatic environments, is still limited. This study presents the multi-niche microbiology of a stranded melon-headed whale ( Peponocephala electra ), including oral, esophageal, foregut, midgut, hindgut, blowhole, and wound (dorsal fin, left anterior, tail). A comparative analysis revealed significant compositional heterogeneity across anatomical niches, with distinct microbial signatures differentiating gastrointestinal, respiratory, and integumentary systems. These findings provide fundamental insights into cetacean microbiome ecology while providing critical baseline information for monitoring health in marine mammal conservation. Microbiome of gastrointestinal Tract of the Peponocephala electra Here, the microbiota structure for the whole GI tract from the oral cavity to the hindgut was analyzed comprehensively, the data revealed significant spatial heterogeneity in microbial diversity across the gastrointestinal tract. Alpha diversity metrics showing a progressive decline in richness along the tract, with the oral exhibiting the highest microbial richness (Sobs = 364 ± 16; Chao1 = 531 ± 53), followed by the esophagus, foregut, midgut, and hindgut. However, statistical comparisons showed that only the oral samples had significantly higher richness compared to other anatomical sites ( p &lt; 0.001). This longitudinal attenuation aligns with observations in striped dolphins ( Stenella coeruleoalba ) and Yangtze finless porpoises ( Neophocaena asiaeorientalis asiaeorientalis ), where oral microbiomes consistently exhibited higher Chao1 richness compared to gut compartments [ 44 , 45 ]. Broader cetacean microbiome studies support this pattern, with gut regions exhibiting varying alpha diversity profiles across species [ 11 , 17 , 46 ]. The principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) revealed two distinct microbial clusters: (1) oral and esophageal microbiomes that are closely related, and (2) gastrointestinal niches (foregut, midgut, hindgut) that form a cohesive cluster. At the phylum level, oral and esophageal communities were dominated by Fusobacteriota, Pseudomonadota, and Bacteroidota, mirroring patterns observed in striped dolphins ( Stenella coeruleoalba ), common dolphins ( Delphinus delphis ), and harbor porpoises ( Phocoena phocoena ) [ 44 ]. Contrastingly, Yangtze finless porpoise oral microbiomes diverged through Bacillota (Firmicutes) dominance [ 45 ], likely reflecting adaptive shifts to freshwater habitats. Even though the richness of microbiota was similar from foregut to hindgut as indicated by the Sob or Chao index, a significant difference in microbiota diversity was found within the intestine (Shannon index: Foregut = 1.77, Midgut = 2.39, Hindgut = 2.25; Foregut vs. Midgut: p = 0.002, Foregut vs. Hindgut: p = 0.015) (Figs. 2 and 3 ). In the meanwhile, significant interspecies variability was identified. In the melon-headed whale, the gut compartments (foregut, midgut, hindgut) were dominated by Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Fusobacteriota. This contrasts with bottlenose dolphins ( Tursiops truncatus ), where Bacillota (Firmicutes) and Proteobacteria predominate in fecal samples [ 27 , 47 – 49 ]. Similarly, Risso’s dolphins ( Grampus griseus ) exhibit Bacillota (Firmicutes)-Fusobacteria-Bacteroidetes-dominant gut communities that may be influenced by starvation-induced dysbiosis [ 50 ]. Baleen whales and sperm whales ( Kogia spp. ) diverge further, with profiles enriched in Bacteroidetes and Bacillota (Firmicutes) that resemble those of both carnivorous and herbivorous species [ 51 , 52 ]. Dwarf minke whales ( Balaenoptera acutorostrata ) harbor communities dominated by Bacillota (Firmicutes) and Actinobacteria, which suggests they have adapted to filter feed [ 53 ]. When compared with the data presented by previous study, we found individual difference in the microbiome composition in the stranded melon-headed whales. In previous study, alternating between Bacillota (Firmicutes)-, Fusobacteriota-, and Bacteroidota-dominant profiles, as well as Proteobactria-dominat profiles was found in the stranded melon-headed whales [ 9 ]. These disparities highlight the combined impact of environmental factors, host phylogeny, and individual physiological states on the development of cetacean gut microbiomes. It is crucial to consider the ecological context, species-specific traits, and individual health status holistically when interpreting marine mammal microbiota data. Notably, Actinobacillus emerged as the second most abundant genus in the foregut microbiota. This finding presents an intriguing paradox given cetaceans’ carnivorous nature, as Actinobacillus has been primarily characterized as a cellulose-degrading microorganism in herbivorous hosts [ 54 ]. However, emerging evidence suggests elevated Actinobacillus proportions may serve as a biomarker for intestinal inflammatory processes [ 55 ]. When considered synergistically with the foregut’s microbial profile - marked by the pathogenic potential of co-dominant Vibrio species and their established inflammatory capacity - the observed Actinobacillus abundance may therefore be attributed to secondary proliferation following Vibrio -induced mucosal inflammation. Microbiome of Respiratory Tract of the Peponocephala electra The respiratory bacterial communities of cetaceans exhibit significant variation among species, reflecting adaptations to their environment. In this study, the blowhole microbiota of a stranded melon-headed whale ( Peponocephala electra ) was dominated by Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota, which contrasts sharply with other body sites based on principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). This distinct phylogenetic profile deviates from patterns observed in other cetaceans. The blowhole communities of Yangtze finless porpoises ( Neophocaena asiaeorientalis ), bottlenose dolphins ( Tursiops truncatus ), and Chinese white dolphins ( Sousa chinensis ) are dominated by Proteobacteria, Bacillota (Firmicutes), and Bacteroidetes, with human care further modifying their composition [ 14 , 56 ]. Further comparative analyses reveal additional taxonomic inconsistencies. Striped dolphins ( Stenella coeruleoalba ) harbor Proteobacteria-Bacteroidetes-Fusobacteria-enriched respiratory microbiota [ 44 ], while Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins ( Tursiops aduncus ) demonstrate Bacteroidetes-Fusobacteria dominance in some studies [ 57 ]. Both common bottlenose dolphins ( Tursiops truncatus ) and their Indo-Pacific counterparts have a predominance of Proteobacteria, Bacillota (Firmicutes), and Fusobacteria, though the temporal stability of these communities remains species-specific [ 16 ]. These variations highlight the impact of host phylogeny, habitat (captive versus wild), and methodological approaches on the respiratory microbiota profiles. The melon-headed whale’s distinctive Pseudomonadota-centric microbial community highlights the possibility of species-specific adaptations to environmental pressures in the marine environment, prompting the need for further research on respiratory microbiome dynamics across cetacean species. Microbiome on the Skin Wounds of the Peponocephala electra Characterizing the wound-associated microbial communities is crucial in understanding infection dynamics, healing processes, and therapeutic strategies. Microbial profiling identifies pathogenic taxa and informs targeted antimicrobial interventions [ 58 , 59 ]. In this study, wound microbiomes exhibit significant alpha diversity variation across anatomical sites, with Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota dominating at the phylum level. This profile was similar to environmental seawater communities (Pseudomonadota: 95.70%; Bacteroidota: 1.94%), suggesting aqueous microbial colonization of skin wounds. Contrasting findings from prior research on Indo-Pacific finless porpoises ( Neophocaena phocaenoides ), where wounds harbored Proteobacteria-, Bacillota (Firmicutes)-, and Bacteroidetes-dominant communities [ 11 ], highlight habitat-driven microbial divergence. Melon-headed whales ( Peponocephala electra ) primarily inhabit tropical and subtropical pelagic zones near deep offshore waters [ 2 ]. In contrast, Indo-Pacific finless porpoises inhabit shallow coastal ecosystems, including estuaries and mangrove swamps. These ecological differences likely influence the assembly of species-specific wound microbiota through differential exposure to environmental microbes. As for the Melon-head whale, multiple potential pathogenic bacteria exhibited significantly higher relative abundances at wound sites than other anatomical locations ( p &lt; 0.05; Supplementary Table S3 ). Three Vibrio species— V. salilacus , V. splendidus , and V. cyclitrophicus —were particularly enriched in left anterior, dorsal fin, and tail wounds, a pattern consistent with potential environmental transmission given the high Vibrio spp. concentrations in surrounding waters. This wound-specific colonization was also observed for V. profundi (left anterior and dorsal fin wounds) and P. tetraodonis (all wound sites plus adjacent water). Notably, some species exhibited dual colonization strategies: Photobacterium damselae maintained gut predominance while accumulating in tail wounds, and Enterococcus faecalis colonized both midgut and tail wound sites. The exclusive wound enrichment of V. salilacus , V. splendidus , V. cyclitrophicus , V. profundi , and P. tetraodonis strongly supports their identification as specialized wound-associated opportunistic pathogens. The Genus Vibrio Genus Vibrio constituted a substantial component of the seawater microbiome, consistent with typical coastal microbial communities. Importantly, the majority of environmental Vibrio strains are non-pathogenic, with only a small fraction posing human health risks [ 60 ]. This prevalence pattern represents a natural feature of marine ecosystems rather than an indicator of contamination. As shown in Fig. 4 , Genus-level analysis revealed the widespread presence of Vibrio across all sampled niches (Fig. 4 ), with seven species identified: Vibrio alginolyticus , V. cidicii , V. cyclitrophicus , V. navarrensis , V. parahaemolyticus , V. salilacus , and V. splendidus . While V. cholerae was detected in most samples, it was absent from esophageal and blowhole communities. Notably, V. profundi was exclusively found on skin wounds (left anterior and dorsal fin). The halophilic species V. alginolyticus , a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen prevalent in warm coastal ecosystems, poses significant zoonotic risks through seafood consumption or aquatic exposure, potentially causing soft tissue infections and systemic complications [ 61 ]. V. cholerae and V. parahaemolyticus , globally recognized seafood-borne pathogens, contribute significantly to human vibriosis outbreaks [ 62 ]. Ecologically specialized species exhibited niche-specific impacts: V. cyclitrophicus causes mortality in juvenile sea cucumbers ( Apostichopus japonicus ) through vibriosis [ 63 ]. Meanwhile, V. navarrensis demonstrates emerging zoonotic potential, as determined by the characterization of clinical isolates [ 64 ]. V. splendidus , a eurytopic species, is a prolific marine opportunist that infects a variety of hosts, including echinoderms and crustaceans. Notably, it has been observed to induce ulcerative dermal syndromes in A. japonicus [ 65 ]. The colonization of the pan-microbiome by Vibrio spp. across multiple anatomical sites in the stranded melon-headed whale suggest systemic microbial invasion, which could indicate immunocompromised status before stranding. Predicted functional profiles of the microbiome A comprehensive analysis of the KEGG Level 1 functional profile revealed enrichment of pathways related to Metabolism, Genetic Information Processing, and Cellular Processes. Notably, the Human Diseases pathway exhibited niche-specific variation, with the highest relative abundance observed in wounds and seawater, which correlates with elevated pathogen prevalence. KEGG Level 3 analysis identified modules associated with virulence, including Vibrio cholerae infection and the pathogenic cycle. These findings suggest the potential for host-pathogen interactions and immune modulation. Such associations are increasingly recognized in marine mammals, where microbial imbalance and pathogenic bacteria have been implicated in stranding events and systemic illness [ 26 , 66 ]. Metacyc Level 2 functional annotations further distinguished niche-specific metabolic specializations, with the gastrointestinal microbiota being enriched for protein digestion/absorption, fatty acid degradation, and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, which is consistent with known dietary and physiological characteristics of cetaceans [ 51 ]. In contrast, blowhole microbial communities exhibited respiratory electron transport chain activation, possibly associated with the oxygen-rich and aerosol-exposed microenvironment. However, it is important to note that the inherent limitations of predictive functional profiling based on 16 S rRNA gene data. Such predictions rely on reference genomes and do not account for horizontal gene transfer, strain-level variation, or actual gene expression, which may result in over- or under-representation of true functional capacity [ 67 ]. As such, while the enrichment of disease-related pathways (e.g., bacterial invasion of epithelial cells, complement evasion) may point to potential risks of immune compromise or inflammation, these predictions require validation through metagenomic or transcriptomic approaches. Nonetheless, the consistent detection of Vibrio -associated pathogenesis modules across wounds and water samples underscores a pressing need to explore the ecological role and health implications of opportunistic pathogens in cetacean strandings and wound healing dynamics. Conclusion In this study, we investigated the microbiome composition in the gastrointestinal tract, respiratory tract, and skin wounds of a stranded melon-headed whale ( Peponocephala electra ). The α diversity results demonstrated significantly higher microbial abundance in anatomical niches directly exposed to the external environment (oral, blowhole, dorsal fin wound and tail wound) compared to internal digestive tract regions (esophagus, foregut, midgut, and hindgut) ( p &lt; 0.05). These findings suggest a pronounced colonization effect of environmental microorganisms in body sites with direct environmental exposure. Notably, Unifrac distances analysis demonstrated high similarity between oral and respiratory microbiomes, indicating bidirectional environment-host interchange dynamics in cetacean mucosal surfaces. This observation also suggests a potential relationship between the frequent interaction of the oral with the external environment and its similarity to the respiratory tract. Concurrently, microbial abundance exhibited elevated levels at all wound sites except for the left anterior wound. The community composition of the wounds demonstrated greater similarity to their living environment, suggesting that exposure of the wounds to the environment may be more conducive to microbial attachment. Finally, multiple potentially pathogenic microorganisms were detected at multiple sites, including multiple species of the genus Vibrio . The presence of these pathogenic microorganisms indicates the potential disease of this melon-headed whale before its death. However, the role these bacteria play in the animal’s death remains uncertain and necessitates further investigation. The microbial composition at the wound site provides critical reference data regarding the capacity of cetacean wounds exposed to the environment for extended periods to resist microbial infestation and facilitate wound healing. However, this study’s reliance on a single stranded individual limits generalizability, as inter-individual variation in cetacean microbiomes is influenced by host genetics, environment, and health status. Consequently, due to the limitation of sample size, further research on the composition of marine mammal microbial communities and potential pathogenic microorganisms is essential for developing effective methods to assess and detect the health status of marine mammals.
